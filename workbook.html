THE HUMAN CHESS PROJECT · chess13

Make a Plan
Study workbook

From positional clues to moves you can explain

24 lessons · Written exercises · Answer key · Six-week route

Attempt each task before reading its answer. Use a physical board whenever it helps you check a line. Online practice: https://knightway8.github.io/chess13/

Suggested pace: four lessons a week, several short practice sessions, and one slow game with review. Use the answer key to diagnose an error, not to skip the attempt.

LESSON 01 · Evaluate before planning

A plan begins after the safety scan

Goal: Keep strategic thought grounded in tactical reality.

Before evaluating a quiet-looking position, check immediate threats and forcing moves. A beautiful long-term plan is irrelevant if a piece can be won now. Once urgent matters are handled, compare king safety, material, piece activity, and pawn structure. This ordering helps a human avoid spending attention on distant possibilities while missing a concrete problem.

Worked explanation

You want to occupy an open file, but the rook move would leave a bishop undefended to a checking capture. Repairing that tactical issue is part of planning, not an interruption of it.

Your task

Describe one position in two stages: urgent facts first, then long-term features.

Watch for: Calling a move positional to avoid calculating its immediate consequences.

Game transfer: Record a move or position from your own game where this idea matters.

LESSON 02 · Evaluate before planning

Compare pieces by jobs, not labels

Goal: Judge activity in relation to the position.

An active piece influences useful squares, has safe mobility, and can contribute to a target. A centralized piece is not automatically active if it can be chased or has no relevant task. Compare corresponding pieces: whose knight has an outpost, whose bishop reaches both wings, whose rook can enter? This produces specific improvement ideas instead of a vague statement that one side looks better.

Worked explanation

A bishop outside its pawn chain may attack nothing useful, while a bishop behind pawns can defend key entry squares. “Outside” and “inside” are descriptions, not complete evaluations.

Your task

Give each of your pieces one current job. Identify the piece with the least useful job and propose a better one.

Watch for: Improving a piece’s appearance while making the rest of the position harder to defend.

LESSON 03 · Evaluate before planning

Material, initiative, and time

Goal: Understand compensation as a claim that needs evidence.

Extra material is valuable because it usually increases options and winning chances. Initiative means making threats that require responses. An initiative can compensate for material only while the threats remain effective; it may disappear after a trade or defensive move. Describe compensation in concrete terms such as exposed king, trapped piece, passed pawn, or development lead.

Worked explanation

A sacrificed pawn may buy open lines against an uncastled king. If the defender safely exchanges queens and completes development, that same pawn deficit may remain without the original compensation.

Your task

For a material sacrifice, list what you receive and which opponent move would reduce it.

Watch for: Assuming activity permanently replaces material regardless of later exchanges.

Game transfer: Record a move or position from your own game where this idea matters.

LESSON 04 · Evaluate before planning

Space is useful when you can use it

Goal: Connect a space advantage to mobility and pawn breaks.

Advanced pawns can give your pieces more room and restrict the opponent. They can also become targets and leave weak squares behind. With more space, preserve enough pieces to exploit mobility while preventing freeing pawn breaks. With less space, useful exchanges and a well-timed break can reduce congestion. The exact trades still depend on which pieces are good and bad.

Worked explanation

A White pawn on e5 may cramp a Black knight, but if Black can strike with ...f6 or ...c5 under favorable conditions, the space advantage may transform quickly.

Your task

Identify the space-gaining pawn, the enemy piece it restricts, and the opponent’s main freeing break.

Watch for: Treating every advance as a gain in space with no defensive cost.

LESSON 05 · Evaluate before planning

Weaknesses need an attacker

Goal: Separate an unusual pawn from an exploitable target.

A weak pawn or square matters when the opponent can attack or occupy it without making a worse concession. An isolated pawn on a closed file may be difficult to reach. A hole near a king can matter immediately if a knight has a route to it. Evaluate access, defenders, and timing before building a plan around a supposed weakness.

Worked explanation

A backward pawn defended by several pieces may tie the defender down even if it cannot be captured. That restriction can be useful, but the attacker still needs an entry route or another target.

Your task

Choose a suspected weakness and identify attacker, route, defenders, and the move needed to begin pressure.

Watch for: Giving a structure a negative name and assuming the game is strategically decided.

LESSON 06 · Evaluate before planning

Make a plan small enough to test

Goal: Convert an evaluation into a short sequence of useful tasks.

A practical plan names a target, a piece that can influence it, a route, and the opponent’s likely resistance. Think in tasks rather than a rigid five-move script. After every move, check whether the assumptions still hold. A plan can be correct even when the opponent prevents it, because provoking a concession may itself improve your position.

Worked explanation

“Pressure the c-file” becomes: place a rook on c1, exchange the blocking pawn if favorable, and seek an entry square. If the opponent contests the file, compare exchanges instead of blindly doubling rooks.

Your task

Write a four-part plan: target, piece, route, resistance. Then choose one move that serves it safely.

Watch for: Confusing commitment to a plan with refusing to update it.

LESSON 07 · Read the pawns

Pawn chains point to breaks

Goal: Identify bases, heads, and contact points.

A pawn chain is a group of diagonally connected pawns. The base often needs piece support because another pawn in the chain does not defend it. Attacking the base can undermine the chain, while attacking the head may open lines near the front. The best break depends on piece readiness and king safety; “attack the base” is a useful question, not an automatic instruction.

Worked explanation

Against White pawns on d4 and e5, Black often considers ...c5 against d4 or ...f6 against e5. Those breaks lead to different files and king-safety consequences.

Your task

In a chain position, mark the base and head, then list one pawn break against each.

Watch for: Playing a thematic break before the pieces can handle the resulting position.

LESSON 08 · Read the pawns

Pawn breaks change the contract

Goal: Evaluate the position that will exist after contact.

A pawn break creates contact with the opponent’s pawns and may open files, diagonals, or squares. Before playing it, calculate likely captures and refusals. Then reassess which pieces improve and which pawns become weak. A break can be strategically correct but tactically premature. Prepare support, remove a pin, or improve a rook before committing if necessary.

Worked explanation

A central ...d5 break may free a bishop and equalize space. If the d-pawn is pinned to a king on d8 or a queen on d7, the same move may fail immediately.

Your task

For a candidate break, draw three outcomes: opponent captures, you capture next, and opponent maintains the tension.

Watch for: Judging the break only by the current structure rather than the structure after exchanges.

LESSON 09 · Read the pawns

Keep or release the tension?

Goal: Make pawn exchanges for a reason.

When pawns attack each other, either side may have a capture available. Maintaining tension preserves options but requires calculation; exchanging clarifies the position and may release the opponent’s pieces. Ask who benefits from the open file, which pawn becomes isolated, and whether the capture improves an enemy piece through recapture. Do not exchange merely because the position feels uncomfortable.

Worked explanation

Capturing a central pawn can give an enemy knight a strong recapture square. Waiting may force the opponent to decide first or allow you to improve a piece before the exchange.

Your task

Compare the position after your capture with the position after one useful developing move that preserves tension.

Watch for: Releasing tension automatically because a capture is available.

LESSON 10 · Read the pawns

Isolated pawns: activity versus targets

Goal: Understand both sides of an isolated-pawn position.

An isolated pawn has no friendly pawn on an adjacent file. An isolated central pawn may provide space and open lines for active pieces, while also becoming a fixed target. The side with the pawn often values activity and favorable breaks; the opponent often seeks a blockade and useful exchanges. These are tendencies, not a verdict independent of the actual pieces.

Worked explanation

A White isolated d4 pawn can support e5 and c5 squares and enable a d5 advance. If Black securely blockades d5 and exchanges active White pieces, the pawn may become harder to defend.

Your task

Give one plan for each side of an isolated d-pawn position. Include the timing of the d5 break.

Watch for: Believing an isolated pawn is always bad or always worth an attacking initiative.

LESSON 11 · Read the pawns

Hanging pawns need coordination

Goal: Read adjacent pawns that lack support from neighboring files.

Hanging pawns are commonly an adjacent pair, often on c- and d-files, without friendly pawns on the outside neighboring files. They can control important central squares and advance with force, but can also become targets. Advancing one may gain space or create a passed pawn while leaving the other backward. Evaluate the transformation rather than treating the formation as permanently good or bad.

Worked explanation

Pushing a d4 pawn to d5 may open a diagonal and establish a passer after exchanges. It may also leave a c4 pawn on an open file. The pieces determine which effect matters more.

Your task

For a hanging-pawn pair, examine each pawn advance and a plan that keeps both pawns together.

Watch for: Pushing a pawn simply to escape pressure without checking what it abandons.

LESSON 12 · Read the pawns

Doubled and backward pawns

Goal: Evaluate defects in context.

Doubled pawns share a file; backward pawns lag behind neighboring pawns and may struggle to advance safely. Such features can reduce mobility and create targets, but the exchanges that produced them may also grant open files, bishop activity, or central control. Count the whole transaction. A structure with an awkward pawn can still be preferable if the pieces become much more useful.

Worked explanation

Accepting doubled c-pawns may open the b-file for a rook and preserve a strong bishop pair. Whether that compensates depends on access to targets and the opponent’s ability to blockade the pawns.

Your task

For one structural concession, list two costs and two possible benefits before evaluating it.

Watch for: Rejecting an exchange solely because it creates doubled pawns.

LESSON 13 · Coordinate the pieces

Open files need entry squares

Goal: Use rooks for a concrete invasion.

An open file has no pawns of either color. A half-open file has no friendly pawn but still has an enemy pawn. A rook on such a file is useful when it can pressure a target, restrict the opponent, or enter on a valuable rank. Occupying a file with no entry squares may accomplish little. Check whether the opponent can contest it and whether exchanges favor you.

Worked explanation

Doubling rooks on the c-file may be strong if c7 is accessible. If every entry square is controlled and the defender can exchange rooks comfortably, another target may be needed.

Your task

Choose a file and name the entry square before selecting a rook move.

Watch for: Putting rooks on an open file as an end in itself.

LESSON 14 · Coordinate the pieces

Outposts are supported access points

Goal: Distinguish a central square from a durable post.

An outpost is a useful square, often in enemy territory, where a piece is supported and difficult to chase with a pawn. A knight may be excellent there if it attacks targets and cannot be exchanged favorably. Check whether enemy pieces can trade it off and whether reaching the square takes too much time. Stability and usefulness both matter.

Worked explanation

A knight on d5 supported by an e4 pawn may look ideal. If Black can exchange it with a bishop and leave White no favorable recapture, the outpost may not provide a lasting advantage.

Your task

For an outpost candidate, list friendly support, enemy pawn challenges, possible piece exchanges, and targets from the square.

Watch for: Assuming a square is permanent merely because one pawn cannot attack it.

LESSON 15 · Coordinate the pieces

Bishops and the pawn-color question

Goal: Evaluate a bishop’s mobility and role.

Pawns on the same color as a bishop can restrict it, especially in a closed center. That bishop may still defend vital squares or become active after a pawn break. A bishop outside its own chain may be active despite same-color pawns behind it. Avoid trading your “bad bishop” automatically if it performs an essential defensive job.

Worked explanation

A light-squared bishop behind light-squared central pawns may be cramped. A timely pawn exchange can open its diagonal, while trading it may leave light squares around the king weak.

Your task

Identify the bishop’s blocked diagonals, current defensive duties, and one plausible freeing break.

Watch for: Trading a restricted bishop without noticing the squares it was protecting.

LESSON 16 · Coordinate the pieces

Improve the worst piece by a real route

Goal: Turn a quiet position into a manageable task.

When no urgent tactic or pawn break dominates, identify your least useful piece and find a better role. Work backward from the desired square to a safe route. Consider whether another piece must move first and whether the opponent can exploit the time spent. The route should fit the pawn structure rather than imitate a maneuver from an unrelated opening.

Worked explanation

A knight may reroute from b1 through d2 to f1 and g3 in one structure, while in another that path is too slow or blocks a rook. Explain the destination’s job before borrowing the route.

Your task

Choose one underperforming piece, a target square, and a two- or three-step route. Test the opponent’s most active response.

Watch for: Maneuvering while the opponent executes an immediate pawn break.

LESSON 17 · Coordinate the pieces

Prophylaxis: ask what they want

Goal: Prevent the opponent’s useful plan without becoming passive.

Prophylaxis means considering the opponent’s intentions and reducing their effectiveness. Start with a concrete candidate plan: a pawn break, an invasion square, or a favorable exchange. A good preventive move often improves your own position too. Trying to prevent every imaginable idea is impossible and wastes time; prioritize the plans that are both strong and feasible.

Worked explanation

A rook move can defend a pawn that would otherwise make a central break possible, while also preparing your own doubling. That is more purposeful than making random pawn moves “for safety.”

Your task

Name the opponent’s best quiet move if you were to pass. Find one response that addresses it and serves your own plan.

Watch for: Using prevention as an excuse to avoid creating threats of your own.

Game transfer: Record a move or position from your own game where this idea matters.

LESSON 18 · Coordinate the pieces

Choose exchanges by the ending they create

Goal: Evaluate trades as transformations.

Before exchanging, picture the remaining pieces and pawn structure. Ask whose worst piece disappears, which king becomes more active, and whether a passed pawn or weak square becomes decisive. Trading when ahead is often sensible because it can reduce counterplay, but an unfavorable pawn ending can throw away an advantage. Keep the specific result ahead of the slogan.

Worked explanation

Exchanging rooks may remove the opponent’s active counterplay but enter a king-and-pawn ending where your king cannot stop an outside passer. Calculate that ending before trading.

Your task

For one proposed exchange, describe the next phase in material, king activity, and pawn weaknesses.

Watch for: Trading automatically because you are a pawn ahead.

LESSON 19 · Convert, defend, and review

Create a second weakness

Goal: Make a stable defender solve two problems.

A single target may be defensible with a compact arrangement. A second weakness can stretch the defender, especially when your pieces can switch wings faster than theirs. First secure your own position and keep pressure on the initial target. Then create or exploit another entry point. The second weakness should require a real concession, not merely add another pawn to a list.

Worked explanation

Pressure on a queenside pawn may tie down a rook. A kingside pawn advance can then create a second target or entry square, provided it does not expose your own king.

Your task

In a position with one defended weakness, identify a second target and the piece that could switch between them.

Watch for: Opening a second front before your own king and first target are secure.

LESSON 20 · Convert, defend, and review

Minority attacks: create a target, then use it

Goal: Understand the purpose behind a familiar pawn advance.

A minority attack uses fewer pawns against a larger enemy pawn group to provoke exchanges and create a weakness. In a typical queenside structure, an advancing b-pawn may induce an isolated or backward enemy pawn. The attack is not simply “push b4–b5”: the center, king safety, and rook placement must support it. The opponent may counter in the center or on the other wing.

Worked explanation

After b4–b5 against a c6-based pawn structure, exchanges may leave c6 weak. If Black can open the center with tempo against an unsafe king, the queenside target may arrive too late.

Your task

Name the desired weak pawn, the exchange that creates it, and the rook file that will pressure it.

Watch for: Repeating a standard pawn pattern in a structure where it creates no useful target.

LESSON 21 · Convert, defend, and review

Convert an advantage by reducing counterplay

Goal: Keep the opponent’s active resources visible.

When better, identify how the opponent could complicate the game: checks, pawn breaks, active rooks, passed pawns, or tactical threats. Remove those resources when you can do so without giving away the advantage. Improve coordination before grabbing every available pawn. A controlled position with a clear conversion route can be more useful than a larger material advantage with an exposed king.

Worked explanation

Before taking a second pawn, moving your king off a checking line may eliminate perpetual-check ideas. That quiet improvement can make the original extra pawn easier to convert.

Your task

List the opponent’s three most active resources. Choose one move that reduces the strongest while preserving your own plan.

Watch for: Relaxing because the engine or material count says you are winning.

Game transfer: Record a move or position from your own game where this idea matters.

LESSON 22 · Convert, defend, and review

Defend with activity and a clear objective

Goal: Seek a playable defense instead of waiting for perfection.

When worse, identify what must be protected and what can be conceded. Passive defense may be necessary briefly, but search for checks, exchanges of the opponent’s strongest piece, freeing breaks, and active rook or king play. A defense should preserve practical choices. Avoid creating irreversible weaknesses just to make a move that looks busy.

Worked explanation

Giving up a pawn to activate a rook behind an enemy passer may improve drawing chances compared with keeping the pawn and remaining tied to a passive defense.

Your task

State the defender’s objective: hold a blockade, trade an attacker, activate a rook, or reach a known drawn ending. Propose a concrete route.

Watch for: Assuming every worse position can be saved by a speculative counterattack.

LESSON 23 · Convert, defend, and review

Review plans at turning points

Goal: Learn from the moment the position changed.

After a game, find the moves that changed the pawn structure, exchanged an important piece, exposed a king, or created a passed pawn. Compare your plan before and after each turning point. Did you update it? Many strategic errors come from continuing a plan whose original conditions no longer exist. Write down the newly available move for each side; this anchors the review in options that actually changed.

Worked explanation

You keep attacking on the kingside after queens are exchanged and the center opens. The more urgent task may now be king activity and control of an open file.

Your task

Choose three turning points from one game. Write the old plan, new board facts, and a revised plan.

Watch for: Reviewing only the largest evaluation drop and ignoring the earlier change that made it likely.

LESSON 24 · Convert, defend, and review

Planning capstone: explain both sides

Goal: Produce a balanced position assessment and a flexible plan.

Choose an unfamiliar middlegame. Complete the safety scan, compare pieces, describe the pawn structure, and identify the most plausible break for each side. Then propose a target, route, and first move. State what response would make you revise the plan. A good explanation is specific enough to test and modest enough to change.

Worked explanation

“I will occupy the open c-file because c7 is an entry square; if the opponent trades rooks and creates a passed pawn, I must reassess the ending before continuing.” This connects features to decisions.

Your task

Write a one-page assessment, play ten training moves from the position, then compare the actual changes with your expectations.

Watch for: Presenting a plan as a secret formula that works regardless of the opponent’s moves.

Answers and recall prompts

01. A plan begins after the safety scan

The first stage should name checks, hanging pieces, and immediate threats. The second can discuss files, weak squares, and pawn breaks.

Recall: When should strategic evaluation begin? After establishing that urgent tactical requirements are understood.

02. Compare pieces by jobs, not labels

The new job must be reachable by a legal route and must not abandon an essential defense.

Recall: What makes a piece active? Useful influence, safe mobility, and a role in the position’s concrete demands.

03. Material, initiative, and time

If you cannot name a concrete benefit or a way to maintain it, treat the sacrifice as unproven.

Recall: Why is initiative temporary? The opponent may neutralize threats and complete the tasks that currently restrict them.

04. Space is useful when you can use it

A useful plan addresses all three. Simply pushing another pawn can enlarge the target rather than improve the position.

Recall: What must the side with more space watch? Pawn breaks that release the opponent’s pieces.

05. Weaknesses need an attacker

If no plausible attacker can reach it, look for a different plan. A weakness can become relevant later after an exchange or pawn break.

Recall: What turns a structural defect into a practical weakness? The opponent’s ability to exploit it.

06. Make a plan small enough to test

Your first move should remain useful if the opponent changes course. If it only works against one cooperative response, it is a variation rather than a robust plan.

Recall: What are the four parts? Target, participating piece, route, and expected resistance.

07. Pawn chains point to breaks

Calculate the first capture choices and identify which pieces benefit from each opening line.

Recall: What decides between attacks on the base and head? The resulting lines, piece activity, timing, and king safety.

08. Pawn breaks change the contract

Name the newly opened line and the new target in each outcome. If one branch is tactically losing, repair it before playing the break.

Recall: What must be compared? The different pawn structures and piece activity after each plausible response.

09. Keep or release the tension?

Choose based on concrete benefits and tactical safety. There is no universal rule that tension should always be kept.

Recall: What is the central question? Whose pieces and structure improve when the tension is resolved?

10. Isolated pawns: activity versus targets

The pawn side should connect activity to a concrete target or break. The blockading side must avoid becoming passive while attacking the pawn.

Recall: What is the main tradeoff? Dynamic piece activity versus a potentially lasting pawn weakness.

11. Hanging pawns need coordination

Name the weak square or file created by each advance and the active opportunity it produces.

Recall: Why is advancing a hanging pawn a major decision? It changes both the pair’s activity and its defensive support.

12. Doubled and backward pawns

A good evaluation names the likely endgame as well as current activity. A temporary benefit may fade after exchanges.

Recall: What should a structural evaluation include? Pawn mobility and targets, plus the activity gained through the transformation.

13. Open files need entry squares

If no entry exists, ask whether a pawn break, exchange, or second weakness can create one.

Recall: What gives file control practical value? Targets, restriction, or a usable invasion square.

14. Outposts are supported access points

A strong post survives the likely responses and contributes to the plan. A decorative knight with no targets is less valuable.

Recall: What must an outpost offer? Durable access and a useful job for the occupying piece.

15. Bishops and the pawn-color question

Choose between improving, exchanging, or retaining it based on the resulting position, not the label alone.

Recall: Does bad bishop mean useless bishop? No; restricted mobility can coexist with an important defensive role.

16. Improve the worst piece by a real route

If the route is too slow, find a shorter improvement or prepare it with a forcing move. Do not confuse a possible route with available time.

Recall: What makes a reroute justified? A useful destination, a safe path, and enough time.

17. Prophylaxis: ask what they want

Check whether the preventive move creates a new weakness. Sometimes allowing the plan and meeting it actively is better.

Recall: What is the first prophylactic question? What useful move would my opponent like to make next?

18. Choose exchanges by the ending they create

If the ending depends on a pawn race or opposition, verify the concrete moves. General evaluation cannot replace that calculation.

Recall: What should determine an exchange? The quality of the position and ending it creates.

19. Create a second weakness

Explain how the defender’s duties conflict. If both targets are easily defended by the same setup, the second target may not stretch anything.

Recall: Why can two weaknesses matter more than one? The defender may be unable to cover both with the same coordinated pieces.

20. Minority attacks: create a target, then use it

Then give the opponent’s central counterplay. A complete plan accounts for both sides’ timing.

Recall: What is the minority attack trying to produce? A lasting target or favorable structural transformation, not merely advanced pawns.

21. Convert an advantage by reducing counterplay

Verify that the move does not permit a tactical reversal. “Simplify” should describe a safer resulting position, not just fewer pieces.

Recall: What is the defender’s most important asset to track? Active counterplay that can change the result.

22. Defend with activity and a clear objective

Calculate the cost of activity. A sacrifice without a resulting defensive mechanism is simply another loss.

Recall: What makes active defense useful? It creates resources while serving a specific defensive objective.

23. Review plans at turning points

Use an engine to test tactics after your own review, and distinguish a bad plan from a tactically flawed execution of a reasonable plan.

Recall: When should a plan be reconsidered? After any meaningful change in structure, material, king safety, or activity.

24. Planning capstone: explain both sides

A useful review identifies which assumptions held, which failed, and one transferable lesson. The objective is better reasoning, not a perfect prediction.

Recall: What is a complete plan? A concrete objective, a feasible route, awareness of resistance, and a reason to revise it.
